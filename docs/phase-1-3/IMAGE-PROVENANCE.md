> Images provided here are believed to be in the public domain and are available under CC0 through Getty’s Open Content Program.

The [Getty Open Content Program](https://www.getty.edu/projects/open-content-program/) independently states:

> Digital images of Getty-owned artworks in the public domain marked with the CC0-public domain icon are available for download under CC0. Such images are not protected by copyright and may be used without restriction or fees for commercial and noncommercial purposes.

This item-specific release is the basis for use. Age or an antique appearance alone was not used as proof of rights. Getty applies a different rights status to some album-level records; the two released image records above were checked individually. Getty's descriptive texts carry CC BY 4.0 separately from the images. PHASM uses its own short descriptions and credits the institution and source pages. No Getty logo or endorsement claim is used.

## Historical interpretation and captions

Sarah Waldorf's [“The Man Who Photographed Ghosts”](https://www.getty.edu/news/the-man-who-photographed-ghosts/), published by Getty on 31 October 2024, explains Mumler's use of double exposures to produce the effects and his marketing of the photographs as spirits. The article reproduces both selected objects. It also records his acquittal after being sued; PHASM does not describe him as convicted.

The current individual collection records give 1869–1878 and 1861–1878 respectively. Those dates take precedence in PHASM over the article's older 1862–1875 captions. The ranges reflect the source's uncertainty, not a known exact day of creation.

Suitable concise display caption:

> Historical spirit photography. A photographic construction, not verified paranormal evidence. William H. Mumler / The J. Paul Getty Museum / CC0.

Where the photographs appear together in a general spiritualist-history feature, the museum links and classification must remain visible. Their proximity to a location story must not imply that the people or spirit effects are connected with that location.

## Image processing and presentation

Each full museum scan was visually inspected. The delivered assets retain the complete photographic card, its original edges, discoloration and black surrounding margin. They were resized proportionally to 735 × 1200 and encoded as WebP quality 83 using Sharp. File sizes are 52,404 and 59,568 bytes respectively. No generative processing, restoration, compositing, object removal, ghost insertion, fabricated damage, or content-aware fill was used.

Any cold monochrome toning or framing used by the interface is CSS presentation. The image files retain the colors of the museum scans. Photographic cards should not be cropped so tightly that the period framing or staged image context disappears. Lazy loading, explicit intrinsic dimensions and local delivery keep these secondary images inexpensive on mobile. Per-file SHA-256 checksums are recorded in the JSON manifest.

## Stanley Hotel research decision

Denver Public Library's [_New Stanley Hotel, Estes Park_](https://digital.denverlibrary.org/nodes/view/1016484) was considered as a potentially relevant early exterior. The library's [Conditions and Image Permissions](https://history.denverlibrary.org/about/conditions-and-image-permissions) require an agreement and assessed fees for commercial/editorial image use. A free, item-specific release was not verified, so the photograph was **not downloaded for use or shipped**.

The Library of Congress has a [public-domain Carol M. Highsmith image of the Stanley Hotel](https://www.loc.gov/item/2015633407/) dated 25 July 2015. It is modern photography, not an early historical record, and was not substituted for an antique photograph. Existing licensed Stanley photographs remain in use with their existing records. No unrelated Victorian portrait is placed in the hotel's gallery or described as a former guest, employee or apparition there.

No image was purchased; no new location profile or review was created.
